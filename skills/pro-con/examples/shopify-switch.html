Pros and cons

Moving a 300-product store from WooCommerce to Shopify

Ten years on WordPress, about 300 products with a few hundred variants, around $600,000 a year across 11,000 orders, and one person who does the maintenance in the evenings. Shopify takes the maintenance away and charges for it in fees, in flexibility, and in one risky quarter.

For Shopify · 10 For staying · 8

One segment per point below, sized by its weight: three for decides it, two for matters, one for minor. A lean, not a landslide. The heaviest con happens once and shrinks with planning; the heaviest pro recurs every month.

Assumed

Organic search brings about 45 percent of orders. The store sells mostly simple products plus one engraved line whose options run on a paid PHP plugin from 2021. Payments go through Stripe today. The peak season is October through December.

The case for Shopify

  • The maintenance goes away

    Core, WooCommerce, the theme, and 23 plugins each update on their own schedule. Two conflicts took checkout down last year, once for a day. Shopify updates itself.

  • A checkout that converts

    Shop Pay has a stored card for a large share of US shoppers, and Shopify claims a low double-digit lift over a plain form. That is their figure, not ours; even a third of it is real money on $600,000.

  • Hosting, speed, and uptime are someone else's job

    The $45 a month server, the caching plugin, the CDN, the certificate renewal, and the backup plugin all go away. Black Friday traffic is Shopify's problem.

  • Costs that are flat and knowable

    Basic at $29 a month on an annual plan plus about $100 in apps, against $45 hosting and about $900 a year in plugin renewals today. Roughly even in dollars, but one invoice instead of eleven and no surprise renewal.

  • Support at 2 a.m.

    Chat support around the clock, against a hosting ticket queue and a plugin author in another time zone.

The case for staying

  • Search traffic takes a hit during the move

    Every product URL changes shape, and so does every category. That is about 340 redirects to map, and stores that skip the map report losing a large share of organic traffic for a quarter. Organic is 45 percent of your orders.

  • The engraving builder has to be rebuilt

    Shopify allows three options per product out of the box. The engraving line's text fields and bundle pricing become either an app at $20 to $50 a month or a rebuild on metafields, quoted at $4,000 to $8,000.

  • Leaving is harder than arriving

    Products, customers, and orders export as CSV; app data, discount history, blog posts, and the theme do not travel. WooCommerce is yours; Shopify is rented.

  • Apps replace plugins, and they rent

    Reviews, wishlist, and bundles run $15 to $60 a month each, the blog is a box rather than a product, and keeping Stripe instead of Shopify Payments adds a 2 percent fee on every order.

Good for

  • A store whose owner does maintenance at night and wants it gone.
  • A catalog of mostly simple products, where the builder is one line and not the whole store.
  • A team that can spend six weeks on a redirect map and cut over in January, after the season.

Bad for

  • A store whose edge is custom PHP: configurators, pricing rules, a link to the warehouse system.
  • A store living on organic search that cannot afford a soft quarter.
  • Anyone for whom the blog is half the traffic. WordPress is the product there; Shopify's blog is an afterthought.

Bottom line

Move, in January, with the redirect map written first.

The pros recur every month and the biggest con happens once. Budget six weeks before cutover for the 340 redirects and a rebuild of the engraving builder on metafields. If that rebuild quote comes back above $8,000, stay on WooCommerce and hire the maintenance out instead; that buys the heaviest pro for about $200 a month without the risky quarter.
