FAQ

Switching to a heat pump in a cold climate

For homeowners where winter reaches zero, heating with oil, propane, or baseboard electric, who have heard both that heat pumps fail in the cold and that they do not. Sixteen questions in four groups; each opens on its own, and the sources sit under every answer.

Does it work

Does a heat pump work below zero?

Yes, if it is a cold-climate model. Units on the NEEP cold-climate list are rated to deliver most of their heat at 5 below and keep running to 15 or 20 below; the ordinary models sold in warmer states are not, and they are where the reputation comes from. Field studies in Maine and Minnesota show homes heated through the whole winter with no backup.

Sources: NEEP list · Field studies

How much heat does it lose in the cold?

Some, and the amount is printed on the spec sheet for every model: typically a quarter to a third of the rated output is gone by 15 below. A good installer sizes the unit to its output at your design temperature, not to the nameplate number, so the drop is planned for rather than discovered.

Sources: NEEP list

Do I need to keep my furnace?

Not if the unit is sized for your design temperature and the house is reasonably tight. Keeping the old furnace through the first winter as a backup costs nothing and is common; owners in the field studies fired it a handful of nights, or never, and most removed it within two years.

Sources: Field studies · Installer guidance

Will it heat the whole house or just one room?

Either, depending on what you install. A ducted unit replaces a furnace and heats through the existing ducts. A ductless system puts one wall head in each zone, and three or four heads heat a typical house. One head in the main room is a partial system that leaves the bedrooms on whatever heated them before.

Sources: DOE guide

Cost and incentives

What does it cost?

Ductless: about $4,000 to $6,000 per head installed, so $12,000 to $20,000 for a whole house. Ducted: about $15,000 to $25,000 to replace a furnace, more if the ducts need work. Quotes for the same job vary by a third between installers, so get three.

Sources: Program cost reports

What incentives are there?

A federal tax credit of 30 percent of the cost, capped at $2,000, for a qualifying unit, claimed on your return. State and utility rebates come on top and range from a few hundred dollars to several thousand; the installer usually files the utility one for you. Check the rebate's own list of qualifying models before you buy, since it is narrower than the federal one.

Sources: IRS credit page · DSIRE

Will my heating bill go down?

Against oil, propane, or baseboard electric, yes, usually by a third or more. Against natural gas it turns on your electricity rate: at about 15 cents a kilowatt-hour the two cost the same to run, and above that gas wins on bills. Run the numbers with the fuel prices on your own last two bills, not a calculator's defaults.

Sources: State calculators, which disagree; see the note in the footer

Does it raise my electric bill a lot?

Yes, in winter. A whole-house system adds roughly the heat the old fuel supplied, delivered at two to three times the efficiency, so the electric bill climbs and the oil or propane bill goes to zero. The net is the saving above. Some utilities offer a heat pump rate that lowers the winter price; ask before the first bill.

Sources: State calculators

Installation

How do I choose an installer?

Ask for a load calculation at your design temperature, and for the model's output at that temperature, in writing. An installer who sizes by square footage or by the old furnace is guessing. Get two or three quotes and prefer the one that asked about your insulation.

Sources: Installer guidance

Does my electrical panel need an upgrade?

Usually not for ductless, which needs one modest circuit per outdoor unit. A ducted whole-house unit with electric backup strips can need a large circuit of its own, which is where a 100 amp panel with an electric range and dryer already on it runs out. The installer's electrician checks this before quoting.

Sources: Installer guidance · DOE guide

Should I insulate first?

If the attic has less than about ten inches, yes. It is cheaper per unit of heat saved than the heat pump, it lets the installer size a smaller unit, and most state programs rebate it too. A drafty house with a heat pump is a warm house with a high bill.

Sources: DOE guide · DSIRE

How long does installation take?

One to two days for ductless, two to four for ducted. The wait for the installer is the longer part: six to ten weeks in the fall, shorter in spring, which is the better season to buy.

Sources: Installer guidance

Living with it

Is it loud?

No. The outdoor unit is about as loud as a refrigerator from a few feet away and quieter than an oil burner; the indoor heads are near silent on low. Placement matters more than the model, so keep the outdoor unit away from a bedroom window.

Sources: NEEP list

What is the defrost cycle?

A few minutes every hour or two in cold, damp weather when the outdoor unit runs in reverse to melt frost off its coil, and the indoor air goes lukewarm. It is normal and nothing is wrong. You will notice it most in the weeks around freezing, less in the deep cold when the air is dry.

Sources: DOE guide

Should I turn it down at night?

No, or by a degree or two at most. A heat pump runs most efficiently holding a steady temperature; a large setback makes it run hard in the coldest hours of the morning to recover. Set it and leave it, which is the opposite of the furnace habit.

Sources: Field studies · DOE guide

How long does it last, and what maintenance does it need?

15 to 20 years, about the same as a furnace. Rinse or vacuum the indoor filters monthly in winter, keep snow and ice off the outdoor unit, and have a technician check the refrigerant every couple of years.

Sources: DOE guide

Still stuck?

Get two quotes from installers on your state program's list and compare their output at your design temperature. If they disagree by more than a ton, the load calculation is the thing to argue about, not the price. Most utility efficiency programs have a free phone line that will read both quotes with you; the number is on the program's rebate page.

Where they differ. The running-cost answers disagree most: the state calculators assume last winter's average fuel prices while installer quotes assume this winter's, and the comparison with gas flips with the electricity rate.
