Recommendation guide

An e-bike for a hilly 14-mile-a-day commute under $2,000

Seven miles each way with two climbs that count, a hard ceiling of $2,000, and a bike a regular shop will put on the stand without sighing. That last condition removed most of the field on its own, and the one bike left standing is not a close call.

Our pick

Buy the Aventon Level 3, about $1,899, because it climbs on a torque sensor and every part on it is one a bike shop already stocks.

Shimano eight-speed drivetrain, Tektro hydraulic disc brakes, a 750-watt rear hub with a torque sensor, a 720-watt-hour battery rated around sixty miles, and fenders, rack, and lights in the box. Aventon sells through independent dealers as well as online, so a shop that does not carry it has still seen one. Price seen this week, before tax and any assembly fee.

This is for you if

  • Your ride has climbs you notice, and you want to arrive without changing shirts.
  • You will keep this bike for years and want a mechanic, not a support ticket, when something wears out.
  • You have ground-floor or elevator storage, or a garage.

Stop reading if

  • You carry the bike up stairs every day. Look at the Velotric below, or at a lighter bike over this budget.
  • Your commute is flat. Half of what you are paying for here is the motor's low-speed pull, and you would not use it.
  • You need it to fold for a train. None of these do.

If not that one

Runner-up

Velotric T1 ST

About $1,549

Wins when weight matters: about 39 pounds with a torque sensor and hydraulic brakes. It gives up the rack, the fenders, and some hill margin.

Budget

Ride1Up 700 Series

About $1,395

Wins when $500 matters more than a dealer. Same class of motor and brakes, cadence sensing instead of torque, and direct-only support.

Shop-backed

Co-op Cycles Generation e1.1

About $1,499

Wins when there is an REI on your route. Service is one counter away; the motor is smaller and the hills will feel it.

There is no upgrade pick. Everything that improves on the Level 3 in a way you would feel on this commute, mid-drive motors from Bosch or Shimano, starts around $2,500 and breaks the budget rather than bending it.

Flaws, not dealbreakers

  • It weighs about 62 pounds with the battery in. One flight of stairs is fine; a daily walk-up is not.
  • The battery is Aventon's own pack, so a replacement in five years comes from them, not from the shop's shelf. The rest of the bike does not share that problem.
  • It ships as a Class 2 with a throttle and can be set to Class 3 in the app. Check which class your city and any trails on the route allow before unlocking it.
  • The included rear rack is rated for about 55 pounds and the fenders are plastic. Both do the job; neither is the part you would choose.

What decided it, in order

The bar is how much each criterion weighed against the first. The order came from your question, not from a spec sheet: you asked about the shop before you asked about the hills.

  1. 1

    Serviceable at a normal shop

    The whole question. Standard Shimano or Tektro parts a mechanic already stocks, a battery that is not glued to the frame, and a brand with dealers. This outweighs everything below.

  2. 2

    Hills without drama

    Two real climbs twice a day means a motor that keeps pulling at low cadence. Torque sensing and a 750-watt-class hub, or any mid-drive, clear the bar; a 250-watt cadence-sensor bike does not.

  3. 3

    Range with margin

    Fourteen miles a day with hills and headwinds needs a real forty-mile rating so a missed charge is an inconvenience, not a walk.

  4. 4

    Weight and stairs

    Anything over sixty pounds is a two-hand carry. It mattered, but less than the three above, and it is where the pick gives ground.

  5. 5

    Fenders, rack, lights included

    Commuting means rain and a bag. Bikes that ship with these save a couple hundred dollars and a shop visit, so it broke ties.

How we chose

Eleven bikes under $2,000 from brands that sell in the United States went on the list. Each was checked for three things a mechanic cares about: named drivetrain and brake parts from Shimano, SRAM, or Tektro; a removable battery; and whether the brand has dealers or a service partner. Six failed at least one and went to the skipped list.

The five left were compared on motor type, sensor type, rated range, weight, and what ships in the box, then ordered by the criteria above. Nothing was ridden for this guide; the hill claim rests on sensor type and motor rating, which is a reliable proxy but not a test. Where a maker's range figure looked optimistic it was discounted by a third.

Considered and set aside

  • Trek FX+ 2. The right bike, about $2,499, which is over the ceiling by more than a sale will close.
  • Rad Power RadCity 5 Plus. About $1,999 and 65 pounds, with a cadence sensor and mechanical brakes at a price that should buy hydraulics.
  • Lectric XPress 750. Strong value at about $1,299, but direct-only, and the class of shops you named will not touch its proprietary display and wiring.
  • Lectric XP 3.0. A folder on 20-inch wheels. Fine for a mile to the station, wrong for seven miles of road twice a day.
  • Specialized Globe Haul ST. A small cargo bike at about $2,700. Superb service network, wrong tool and wrong price.
  • Priority Current. Mid-drive and belt-driven, the nicest ride here, at about $3,299.
