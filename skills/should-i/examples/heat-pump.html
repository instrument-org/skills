Should I…?

Should I replace the oil furnace in my 1970s Vermont house with a heat pump?

A house built when oil was cheap and insulation was optional, a furnace in its third decade, and winters that reach 20 below. That sounds like the case against. It is the case for, because the furnace is due whether or not you change fuels, and only the extra money has to earn itself back.

Yes

With a cold-climate unit sized at your design temperature, after the attic is insulated, and with the oil furnace kept through the first winter as the backup you will probably never use.

What would flip it: a furnace under ten years old, or a 100-amp panel with no room and no budget to change it.

What the answer assumes

An 1,800 square foot house with a basement and ducts, 750 gallons of oil a year at $3.90, a furnace installed around 2000, six inches of attic insulation, and a 200-amp panel. That house spends about $2,900 a year on oil. A ducted cold-climate heat pump heats it for about $2,100 in electricity at 21 cents a kilowatt-hour, and cools it in July for the first time. It costs about $18,000 installed, $14,000 after the federal credit and the state rebate; a new oil furnace would cost $7,500. The extra $6,500 comes back in about eight years at today's oil price, and in five at last winter's. Where your house differs, the branches below say what changes.

What it depends on

  • Ducts

    If your ducts are sound, a ducted cold-climate unit replaces the furnace in place and the house feels the way it does now, with cooling added.

    If they leak, or the upstairs was always cold, put ductless heads in the three rooms you live in, keep the ducts for the backup, and spend about $3,000 less.

  • Attic

    If the attic has less than ten inches of insulation, do that first: $3,000 to $4,500, about half of it rebated, and it takes 15 percent off the heat load, which is the difference between a four-ton unit and a three.

    If it already has fourteen inches or more, skip this and size the unit to the house as it is.

  • Panel

    If the panel is 200 amps, a three-ton unit takes a 30-amp breaker and two free slots, and nothing else changes.

    If it is 100 amps with an electric range and a dryer already on it, budget $2,500 to $4,000 for a panel upgrade before anyone quotes a heat pump, and add a year to the payback.

  • Design day

    If your design temperature is about 10 below, which covers the Champlain valley, any unit that publishes full output at 5 below and most of it at 15 below will do.

    If it is 20 below or colder, the Northeast Kingdom and the high towns, insist on a unit rated for output at 22 below and a load calculation run at that number, not at the 5 above a standard install uses.

  • Years left

    If you will be in the house eight years or more, the extra $6,500 is paid back and the cooling is free after that.

    If it is under five, the bills do not return it and buyers pay for perhaps half; the case rests on the furnace being due anyway, which is thinner.

By situation

If your furnace is over 20 years old Yes, now

You are buying a heating system this decade regardless, and the week it dies in January is the worst week to shop. The extra $6,500 buys a lower bill and the first air conditioning the house has had.

If your furnace is under 10 years old Not yet, but start

Put two ductless heads in the rooms you live in, about $8,000 net, and run them from October to December, March to May, and all summer. Let the furnace do January. Replace it with the ducted unit when it fails.

If you cool with window units Yes, and count the cooling

The heat pump retires them too: about $300 a summer in electricity, and the lifting. Add it to the saving and the payback drops to about six years.

If the budget is tight Yes, in pieces

Attic first, about $1,800 net. Then one head in the main room, about $4,500 net, which carries the shoulder seasons alone. Then the furnace when it fails, with the load already 15 percent smaller.

What goes wrong

  • Sized for the wrong day. An installer who sizes for 5 above sells a unit that runs flat out and falls behind at 15 below. Ask for the load calculation and the unit's rated output at your design temperature, on paper, before you sign.
  • No backup and a power cut. The furnace needs power too, so this is not new, but a heat pump with nothing behind it makes a generator or a wood stove part of the plan.
  • Electric rates. A 20 percent rise takes a third of the $800 saving. Oil has moved more than that in a single year, so the risk runs both ways.
  • Placement. The outdoor unit hums, and it drips when it defrosts. Keep it off the bedroom wall and off the walkway that ices.
  • The installer. More of the outcome than the brand. Choose by references from houses like yours, not by the lowest quote.

Next step

Book a home energy audit through the state efficiency utility

It costs $100 to $300, often rebated back, and takes a morning. It produces the load calculation and the rebate paperwork a good installer wants to see, and it tells you whether the attic goes first. Then get three quotes that each name the model, its output at 15 below, and the load figure it was sized to. If two of the three agree within half a ton, sign the one with the better references.

Questions people ask

Will it keep the house warm at 20 below?

A cold-climate unit sized at the design temperature keeps up. Below it, output falls and the backup carries the last few degrees on the handful of nights a year that go there.

Should I keep the oil furnace?

For the first winter, yes, as backup. Most households in the field studies fire it a few nights and remove it after a year or two, when the tank's next fill comes due.

What about ground-source instead?

$30,000 to $45,000 with the well, better efficiency, and 15 years or more to pay back the difference. Worth it only if the yard is already being dug up.

Will my electric bill double?

It rises about $2,100 a year over what you pay now, and the oil bill goes to zero. The net is the $800 saving, most of it landing between December and February.

Why not propane?

Same truck, same price swings, same 20-year furnace. It solves nothing the heat pump does not, and it does not cool the house.
