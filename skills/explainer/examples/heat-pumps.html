Explainer

How a heat pump heats your house in winter

The installer said it moves heat instead of making it, and that it keeps working below zero. Both are true, neither explains anything, and you are about to spend furnace money on a box that looks like an air conditioner. Here is what that box is doing on a cold morning.

The short version

A heat pump is an air conditioner that can run in reverse. In winter it pumps a refrigerant around a loop: the outdoor coil lets the refrigerant soak up heat from the cold air, the compressor squeezes it until it is hotter than your rooms, the indoor coil hands that heat to the air your ducts carry, and a valve drops the pressure so the refrigerant is cold again and ready for more. Because it moves heat rather than burning fuel to make it, it delivers two to three units of heat for every unit of electricity it uses, and that ratio falls, but does not vanish, as the outside air gets colder.

How it works

Four things happen, in a loop

  1. 1

    The outdoor coil soaks up heat

    Cold air still holds heat: the refrigerant in the coil boils at a temperature far below any winter morning. A fan pulls outside air across the coil, the refrigerant inside boils into a vapor, and the air leaves a few degrees colder than it arrived.

  2. 2

    The compressor makes it hot

    The vapor is warm, but not warmer than your living room, so it cannot give heat to it yet. The compressor squeezes the vapor to a much higher pressure, and squeezing a gas heats it, the way a bicycle pump warms in your hand. This is where the electricity goes.

  3. 3

    The indoor coil gives it away

    The hot vapor runs through the coil in your air handler. The blower pushes room air over it, the air warms, and the refrigerant, having given up its heat, condenses back to a liquid. Your ducts carry the warm air to the rooms.

  4. 4

    The valve makes it cold again

    The liquid is still under high pressure. The expansion valve lets it through a narrow opening into the low-pressure side, and the sudden drop makes it very cold, colder than the outside air, so it can soak up heat on the next pass. The loop begins again.

What it is

An air conditioner with one extra part

Your air conditioner already runs this loop every summer, taking heat from the house and dumping it outside; the refrigerator in your kitchen runs it too, taking heat from the food and dumping it into the room. A heat pump adds a reversing valve that swaps which coil does which job, so in winter the outdoor coil is the one absorbing and the indoor coil is the one releasing. The same machine cools in July and heats in January, which is why one replaces both a furnace and an air conditioner.

The difference from a furnace is where the heat comes from. A furnace makes heat by burning fuel, so one unit of fuel gives at most one unit of heat. A heat pump moves heat that already exists in the outside air, and the electricity only runs the compressor and the fans. That is how it gives back more heat than the electricity it draws.

Why it matters to you

What the loop means for the quote on your table

  • Ask for the capacity at your design temperature, not the nominal size. Every heat pump is rated at 47°F. The number that matters is how much heat it delivers on the coldest night your area plans for, and the installer's load calculation should state both.

  • Look for a cold-climate model. Its compressor runs at variable speed and it is rated for output at 5°F; a standard unit loses much of its capacity well before then, because step 1 has less heat to work with as the air gets colder.

  • Decide on backup before you decide on size. A unit sized for the coldest night is oversized for the rest of the winter. Many installers size for most nights and keep a small electric strip or the old furnace for the few below that; ask which the quote assumes.

  • Expect the vents to feel different. The air is warm rather than hot, and the unit runs for long stretches at a low speed, which is how it is meant to work. A thermostat set back at night and pushed up in the morning fights that and costs more.

What people get wrong

You might think

It stops working once it is below freezing outside.

In fact

Air holds heat at any temperature you will ever see, and the refrigerant boils far below freezing. What falls as it gets colder is how much heat the unit can move per hour, which is why the rating at 5°F, not the one at 47°F, is the number to compare.

You might think

Bigger is safer, so round the size up.

In fact

An oversized unit reaches the thermostat's setting quickly, shuts off, and starts again minutes later. Short cycles wear the compressor, leave far rooms cold, and cost more than a right-sized unit running steadily. The load calculation exists to prevent exactly this.

You might think

The outdoor unit steaming or dripping in winter means it is broken.

In fact

The outdoor coil is colder than the air, so frost forms on it in damp cold. The unit briefly runs the loop backward to melt it, which is the steam, and pauses the blower so it does not push cold air into the house. A few minutes an hour in the right weather is normal.

You might think

It heats with electricity, so it costs what electric heat costs.

In fact

Electric resistance heat gives one unit of heat per unit of electricity. A heat pump gives two to three, because most of the heat comes from the outside air and the electricity only runs the compressor and the fans. Your bill depends on that ratio at your temperatures and your electricity price, which is a calculation worth doing before you sign.
