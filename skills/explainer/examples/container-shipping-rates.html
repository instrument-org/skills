How ocean container shipping rates are set

You asked three forwarders for a price on one 40-foot container from Ningbo to Los Angeles and got three numbers that do not agree, one of them valid only until the fifteenth, and one with a line called PSS that nobody explained. The numbers are not wrong. They are answering slightly different questions, and this page is about what those questions are.

The short version

A container rate is the price of one box, one direction, one lane, in a window of a few weeks. Underneath it is a base ocean freight figure that moves with how full the ships are that week, and on top of it is a stack of surcharges the carriers add for fuel, season, and ports. Big shippers lock the base in yearly contracts; everyone else buys at the spot price, and small importers buy it through a forwarder who has bought space in bulk and resells it with a margin. Your three quotes differ because each forwarder holds different space, itemizes differently, and expires on a different date.

What a rate is

The nearest familiar thing is an airline fare. The seat costs whatever the airline thinks it can get this week for this flight, the base fare is only part of the ticket, and the person next to you paid something else. A container slot works the same way, with two differences. The ship sails whether or not your box is on it, so an empty slot is worth nothing to the carrier after departure, which is why the price moves so hard with demand. And almost nobody buys a slot from the carrier directly, so the price you see has passed through at least one reseller on the way to you.

How the number gets built

  1. 1

    Capacity is fixed months ahead. Carriers run ships on weekly loops, called services, between a fixed string of ports. The number of slots on a lane in a given week was decided when the ships were assigned to the service, so in the short run supply cannot move. Only the price can.

  2. 2

    The base rate follows the balance of boxes to slots. When more cargo wants to move than the week's slots can carry, the base rises; when ships sail light, it falls. Three published indexes, the Freightos Baltic Index, Drewry's World Container Index, and the Shanghai Containerized Freight Index, track the going spot rate on the main lanes each week, and carriers and forwarders both watch them. A quote that has moved since last month has usually moved with the index.

  3. 3

    Surcharges are stacked on the base. A fuel surcharge that resets with bunker prices. A peak season surcharge, the PSS on your quote, in the months before the holidays. Terminal handling charges at each end. A general rate increase, which is a carrier announcing that the base goes up on the first or the fifteenth of the month, and which sticks only if the ships are full enough that shippers pay it. Some of these are on every quote and some appear only on some lanes, and all of them are why "ocean freight" and "the total" are different numbers.

  4. 4

    Contracts fix the base for the big shippers. Large importers sign annual contracts, mostly in the spring for the trans-Pacific, at a base rate that holds for a year in exchange for a volume commitment. Surcharges still float. When the spot market falls below the contract, they push to renegotiate; when it rises above it, the carrier finds reasons to roll their boxes to a later sailing.

  5. 5

    Forwarders buy space and resell it. A forwarder, or an NVOCC, which is the licensed form of one, holds its own contracts with carriers, buys spot space when it needs more, adds its margin, and quotes you one price. This is where a small importer's rate is actually decided: by which carriers this forwarder has space with on your lane this month, and how much margin it will give up for your business.

  6. 6

    The quote has an expiry date for a reason. Because the base can reset on the first and the fifteenth, quotes run to one of those dates. A quote is a price for a booking made before it expires, on a sailing inside its window. A container that misses its sailing is repriced at whatever the number is then.

  7. 7

    Ocean freight is not the whole bill. Trucking at origin, export clearance, the customs entry and duty at destination, the chassis that moves the box off the terminal, and the daily charges for keeping the box or the chassis past its free days are all outside the ocean rate and inside what you pay. Two forwarders quoting the same ocean rate can be far apart on the door-to-door total.

What this means for your three quotes

  • Compare the total to the door, not the ocean line. Ask each forwarder for the same scope: pickup at the factory, delivery to your warehouse, customs entry in or out.
  • Ask which lines are fixed and which float. Fuel and peak surcharges can change between the quote and the sailing; a forwarder who says so unprompted is telling you something about the others.
  • Match the validity dates. A quote good to the fifteenth against one good to the thirtieth is not a fair comparison in a month with an increase announced.
  • Know your free days. Demurrage and detention are where a good rate becomes a bad shipment. Ask how many free days at the destination terminal and for the container itself, and what a day costs after that.
  • At your volume, the forwarder's contracts are your contracts. Choose one who is strong on your lane rather than one who is cheap this month.

What people get wrong

"The cheapest quote is the cheapest shipment."

In factThe three quotes almost certainly do not cover the same scope. One includes terminal handling at both ends, one includes the customs entry, and one includes neither and shows the lowest number. Line them up on the same scope before comparing, and the order often changes.

"The shipping line sets the price."

In factThe line publishes a tariff and announces increases, but the market decides which increases hold, and the forwarder decides what you pay. The line's price is the opening position in a negotiation you are not in the room for.

"A quote is good until the ship sails."

In factIt is good until its validity date, for a booking made by then. Book inside the window and the rate holds for that sailing; wait a week past it and you are on next month's number, whatever that turns out to be.

"More volume is the only way to a better rate."

In factConsistency, flexibility on sailing dates, cargo that is easy to handle, and paying on time all earn a forwarder's better space. A small importer who ships every month on a predictable lane is worth more to a forwarder than a large one who shows up twice a year at peak.

Five terms on your quotes

FCL
Full container load: you fill a whole box. The alternative, LCL, shares a box with other shippers and is priced by volume.
NVOCC
Non-vessel operating common carrier: a forwarder licensed to issue its own bill of lading and hold its own contracts with carriers.
THC
Terminal handling charge: what the port charges to lift the box on or off the ship, billed at each end.
GRI
General rate increase: a carrier's announced increase to the base rate, usually dated the first or the fifteenth, and only real if the market accepts it.
Demurrage and detention
Daily charges for a box left at the terminal past its free days (demurrage) or kept outside the terminal past its free days (detention). The part of the bill nobody quotes.
