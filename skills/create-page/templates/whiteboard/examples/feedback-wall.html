Whiteboard · Cancellation survey, Q3

Why 36 subscribers left, on one wall

Every free-text answer from the exit survey, one to a note, sorted into the six things people were actually saying. Sorting is the whole argument: a list of 36 quotes is unreadable and a bar chart of six categories throws away the sentences that made the categories worth having. Drag a note if you disagree with where it sits — several of them are genuinely arguable, and the four in the last group would not sit anywhere at all.

How to read the wall

Six dashed regions, each holding the notes that are making the same point. A note in the middle of nothing is a note nobody could place.

What the arrangement says that the six counts do not

The biggest group is also the most recoverable

Nine people left over price, and every one of them who answered said they would consider coming back. Read the notes rather than the count and only two are about the number itself: the rest are about finding out from a card statement, not knowing the per-cup cost, and a pause that behaved like a cancellation. Three of those are billing-page problems, not pricing problems.

The cadence group is one missing option

Seven people said some version of “too much coffee”. Four of their notes name a specific interval that does not exist, or a delivery that should have noticed the last one was untouched. This is the group where the fix is smallest and the wall makes that visible in a way the category label hides.

Quality is small, and it is the one that does not come back

Five notes, and it is the only region where the white notes outnumber the green. People who leave over price are annoyed. People who leave because the roast dates slipped from four days to eleven have concluded something, and the survey suggests they do not un-conclude it.

Four notes would not sit anywhere, and that is a finding

Bottom right. Someone felt guilty about the packaging. Someone cancelled every subscription they had. Someone did not know their household had cancelled it. Someone could not remember. A tidy chart would have distributed these four into the nearest category and quietly overstated all six.

Where these came from

  • The wall is illustrative. The quotes, the subscriber numbers, the tenures and the counts on this page are invented for the purpose of showing what an exit survey looks like when it is sorted rather than counted. No real survey lies behind it and no real business is described.
  • One note is one answer, quoted, not summarized. That is the rule this format depends on: the moment notes become paraphrases the wall stops being evidence and becomes an opinion with a layout.
  • Sorting is a judgment and several are arguable. “Bought a bigger grinder” sits under cadence and could sit under life. “Support fixed it quickly both times” is filed under delivery and is arguably praise. The drag exists so a reader can act on disagreeing rather than only note it.
  • Anyone who left without answering is not here. Which is most of them. A wall of the people who did explain themselves is not a picture of everyone who left, and the six counts should never be read as shares of anything.
An illustrative wall: every quote, tenure and count on it was written to show the shape of a sorted exit survey, not gathered from one. What the page is really arguing is a method — that free-text answers should be kept whole and placed, and that the notes which refuse to be placed are worth more than the ones that fall neatly into a bucket.
