Whiteboard · Sign-in outage, 21:14 to 22:04

Fifty minutes, laid out end to end

The same evidence a post-mortem document would carry — log lines, a graph, what support was hearing, what we said to each other — but placed at the minute it happened and in the lane it belongs to. The argument the layout makes and a document cannot is about the gaps: five minutes between the first failure and the first alert, twenty-two between the alert and anyone opening the right graph, and a rollback at 21:38 sitting directly above the moment it doubled the failure rate.

Sign-in starts failing

The lock queue reaches the connection pool. Anyone signing in gets a spinner and then a generic error. Sessions already open keep working, which is why nobody in the building noticed.

21:23

First alert fires

Error rate above 2% for 4 minutes. Paged the on-call. Nine minutes after the first failed sign-in.

21:31

Wrong first hypothesis

The graph everyone opens shows the API, and the API was fine. Twenty-two minutes went into the load balancer before anyone looked at the database.

“LB looks healthy. Rolling back the 20:40 deploy to be safe.”

21:38

The rollback made it worse

Rolling back cycled every application node, so every in-flight connection re-queued behind the same lock. Failure rate went from 40% to 91% in two minutes, and held there for sixteen.

21:44

Support queue triples

31 tickets in eleven minutes, all saying the same thing in different words. Support had no way to know an incident was open.

“Is it just me or is login down? Third try.”

21:53

Someone opens the database dashboard

Thirty seconds later the cause is obvious. Total time from the first alert to the right graph: thirty minutes, and none of it was hard.

What we changed the same night

  • Rebuild moved behind a lock timeout of 20 seconds.
  • Database saturation added to the first-response dashboard.
  • Support gets the incident channel automatically.

23:10

What we did not change

Rolling back a deploy is still the first reflex, and on this night it doubled the blast radius. Nobody has a proposal for that yet, and it is the most likely cause of the next one.

Four things you can only see by looking across the lanes

Customers were failing for nine minutes before anything paged

The bottom lane starts at 21:19 and the middle lane starts at 21:23, and there is nothing above either of them until 21:16. The alert threshold is a four-minute average of a rate that took five minutes to climb, which means the detector is structurally about nine minutes behind the experience. That is not a tuning problem, it is the wrong signal.

The rollback sits directly above the worst two minutes

21:31 in the middle lane, 21:38 in the top. Cycling every node put every connection back in the same queue and took failures from 40% to 91%. On a written timeline these are two bullet points seven minutes apart. Stacked, the causation is the layout.

Twenty-two minutes went into the wrong graph

21:31 to 21:53 in the middle lane is a single hypothesis, and the thing that ended it was somebody opening a dashboard nobody had opened all night. Thirty seconds after that, the cause was obvious and the fix took four minutes. The expensive part of this outage was not the failure and not the fix.

Support found out from customers

31 tickets by 21:44 and no card anywhere in the middle lane telling them an incident was open. Of the three changes made that night, the cheapest is the one that would have taken the most weight off the people who were not in the room.

What this is made of

  • The incident is illustrative. The log lines, timestamps, ticket counts, failure rates and quotes were written for this page to show what an incident looks like when it is laid out rather than written down. No real outage is described.
  • Time is to scale and that is load-bearing. Twelve pixels a minute everywhere, so a wide gap is a long gap. A canvas that spaces cards for the layout rather than by the clock reads identically and means nothing, which is the way this format goes wrong.
  • Nothing is placed by feel. Every card sits at a timestamp printed on its own face, so a reader who thinks a card is in the wrong place can check it against its own label rather than trusting the drawing.
  • Not on the canvas: the deploy at 20:40 that got rolled back, anything about who was on call, and the cost. The first belongs here and is missing because the log was rotated before anyone looked.
An illustrative incident: every timestamp, log line, count and quote on this canvas was written for the page rather than taken from an outage, and the four findings are readings of the drawing rather than conclusions anyone reached. What is real is the claim about the format — that the expensive parts of an incident are gaps between lanes, and that a document written in paragraphs has nowhere to put a gap.
