Three things the drawing makes obvious and the spreadsheet did not

Support and Engineering are as far apart as the floor allows

Engineering is the top-left block; Support runs along the bottom middle. The two teams that escalate to each other several times a day are now separated by the full width of the floor and by the kitchen. Nothing in the spreadsheet said this, because a spreadsheet has no geometry. Swapping Support with Sales would cost twelve moves and fix it.

Every hybrid-capable room is on the same side

The Long Room and the Kiln are the only two rooms with a screen and a camera, and both are in the top-right block. Anyone in Design taking a call with the New York office crosses the whole floor to do it, past the two teams whose work is loudest.

The eight unassigned desks are all in one corner

Three in Engineering, three in Design, two in Support, and every one of them at the outer edge of its block. If those are meant to be the hot desks for the people who come in twice a week, they are the worst seats in the building and they are also the ones nobody chose. Scattering them through the blocks costs nothing now and cannot be done once the furniture is bolted down.

What this is drawn from

  • The plan is illustrative. The floor, the zones, the room names and all 96 people on it were written to show what a seating plan looks like when it is drawn rather than tabulated. No real office is described and none of these are real people.
  • Drawn to scale, or it is worthless. The single thing this format offers over a list is that distance means distance. A plan whose blocks are sized for the layout rather than for the building looks the same and tells you nothing, which is worse than a spreadsheet because it looks authoritative.
  • Names appear at about sixty per cent and above. That is a rendering choice, not a secret: the same names are in the file whether or not the page is zoomed in, and a reader with no script gets the whole floor at full size with every label showing.
  • Not drawn: the first floor, the stairs and lift core, fire exits, and anything about desk heights or accessibility. The last of those has to be on the plan before it is agreed, and its absence here is a gap rather than a decision.
An illustrative plan. The floor, the teams, the rooms and every name on it were written for this page rather than taken from a building, and the three findings are arguments the drawing supports rather than measurements anyone took. What is real is the method: a seating plan is a spatial document, and the questions worth asking about one are questions about distance that a table of names cannot be asked.
