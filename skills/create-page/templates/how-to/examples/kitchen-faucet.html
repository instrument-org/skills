How-to guide

Replace a kitchen faucet yourself

An afternoon on your back under the sink, and the only specialty tool costs less than lunch. The old faucet comes off harder than the new one goes on, so the middle of this guide is slow and the end is quick. Written for a sink with shutoff valves under it and a faucet that mounts from below, which is nearly every kitchen faucet sold in the last thirty years.

What to have on the floor before you start

Tools

  • Basin wrench. Long shaft, swiveling jaw, about $15. It is the only tool that reaches the nuts behind the sink bowl.
  • Adjustable wrench, medium, for the supply line nuts.
  • Headlamp. Both hands will be busy.
  • Bucket, old towels, a small bowl for the parts you take off.
  • Plastic putty knife and a scouring pad, for the ring the old faucet leaves.
  • Your phone, to photograph the connections before you undo them.

Materials

  • The new faucet, with the supply lines and gasket in its box. Most include them; check the box before you leave the store.
  • Two new supply lines, braided stainless, only if the faucet came without them. Match the valve end, usually 3/8 inch compression.
  • Plumber's tape, one roll. You will use a few inches of it, or none.
  • Penetrating oil, for the nuts that have been wet for a decade.

Check before buying

  • Count the holes in the sink: one, three, or four. A one-hole faucet fits a three-hole sink with the deck plate in its box; the reverse does not work.
  • Measure the space above the sink. A tall gooseneck under a low window is the most common return to the store.

Stop and call a plumber if

  • There are no shutoff valves under the sink, only pipe. You would have to shut off the whole house and the job becomes a different job.
  • A valve will not turn, or keeps dripping from its stem once it is closed. Forcing it snaps the stem, and then the water is on until someone with a torch arrives.
  • The pipes coming out of the wall are gray galvanized steel and flake when touched, or anything is already wet before you start.

The steps

  1. 1 10 min easy

    Empty the cabinet, shut both valves, and open the old faucet.

    Everything under the sink comes out, the towels go in, and the bucket goes under the valves. Turn each valve clockwise until it stops, without forcing the last quarter turn. Then lift the old faucet's handle to full hot and full cold: what drains out is what was in the lines, and it should slow to nothing within a few seconds. If it keeps running, a valve is not holding, and that is the first item in the red box above.

  2. 2 10 min easy

    Photograph everything, then disconnect the supply lines at the valves.

    Take the photos before touching anything: which line goes to which valve, where the sprayer hose runs, where the weight hangs. Then hold each valve still with one hand and turn the supply nut counterclockwise with the wrench. A cup of water comes out of each line; that is the bucket's job. Leave the lines attached at the faucet end, since they come out with it.

  3. 3 20 to 40 min hard

    Free the old faucet from underneath. This is the slow part.

    Lying on your back with the headlamp on, find the mounting nuts on the faucet's threaded shanks, up behind the bowl where a normal wrench cannot go. The basin wrench's jaw hooks onto a nut and the long handle gives you the leverage from down by your chest. Turn counterclockwise. Once the nuts are off, go up top and lift the faucet out, feeding the old lines up through the holes.

    When a nut will not move

    Soak it with penetrating oil, go make coffee, and try again in fifteen minutes; it works far more often than it seems it should. Plastic mounting nuts on a cheap old faucet can be cut with a hacksaw blade wrapped in tape, carefully, since the sink is right there. Never heat anything under a sink.

  4. 4 10 min easy

    Scrape the deck clean and dry it.

    The old faucet leaves a ring of hardened putty or a gasket's ghost. The plastic putty knife lifts it without scratching stainless or enamel; the scouring pad takes the rest. Dry the deck completely, because the new gasket seals against a clean flat surface and nothing else.

  5. 5 15 min moderate

    Set the new faucet, then tighten it from below by hand.

    Fit the rubber gasket to the base (or the deck plate, if you are covering three holes), feed the new lines and the sprayer hose down through the hole, and have someone hold it straight from above while you thread the mounting nut up from below. Hand tight, then a quarter turn with the basin wrench. Check from above that the spout is centered on the sink before that quarter turn, because it does not move afterward.

  6. 6 10 min easy

    Connect the supply lines: hot on the left, hand tight plus a quarter turn.

    Hot is the left valve as you face the cabinet, and the faucet's hot line is usually marked red or sits on the left as you look at the spout. Thread each nut on by hand until it stops, then a quarter turn with the wrench while the other hand holds the valve still. Then hang the sprayer weight on the hose where your photo shows the old one sat.

    Where the tape goes, and does not

    The nuts on braided supply lines seal with a rubber washer inside; tape on those threads keeps the washer from seating and makes the leak you were trying to prevent. Tape belongs only on a bare tapered pipe thread, which on this job is usually nowhere. If a joint weeps after a quarter turn, another eighth turn is the fix, not tape.

  7. 7 15 min easy

    Unscrew the aerator, open the valves slowly, and run it for a full minute.

    Take the aerator off the spout tip first, so the grit from the new lines flushes out instead of lodging in it. Open each valve a little, then all the way, watching the joints. Run hot and cold for a minute each, then screw the aerator back on. Dry every joint with a paper towel and look again after ten minutes; a leak shows as a bead, not a drip, at first.

If your sink is different

  • A garbage disposal is in the way. Unplug it at the outlet before step 1 and work around it; taking it off is a separate job with its own surprises, and it rarely has to come off for a faucet.
  • A pull-down sprayer. In step 5, the hose has to hang free below the deck for its whole travel; a P-trap or the disposal's cord in its path makes the sprayer stick halfway. Route the hose before you tighten the mount.
  • Three holes, one-hole faucet. The deck plate goes on in step 5 with its own gasket underneath, and the two spare holes end up under it. Some deck plates ask for a bead of silicone instead; the sheet in the box wins.
  • Old-style rigid supply tubes (chrome, bent by hand) instead of braided lines. Replace them with braided lines in step 6 rather than reusing them; they are the $10 that saves a second afternoon.
