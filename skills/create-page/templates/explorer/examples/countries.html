Three things the data says, one of them about itself

Seven countries hold half the world.

India, China, the United States, Indonesia, Pakistan, Nigeria and Brazil come to just over four billion people between them. The other 210 rows on this page share the rest. The top ten are 57% of everybody, which means a chart of “countries” is nearly always a chart of ten of them and a long tail that rounds to nothing.

The income ladder is seventeen and a half years of life.

Median life expectancy runs 81.3 years in high-income countries, 74.5 in upper middle, 67.9 in lower middle and 63.8 in low income. The median output per person across the same four rungs runs 39,041 dollars to 880 — a factor of forty-four for a difference of seventeen years, which is the shape of the scatter above: steep at the bottom and almost flat once a country is comfortable.

A region here is not a size, and two of them prove it.

South Asia is six countries and 1.69 billion people. Sub-Saharan Africa is forty-eight countries and 1.32 billion. Grouping by region tells you about the World Bank’s filing system as much as about the world, so any per-region average on this page is an average of wildly unequal things. Group by income below and the boxes get more comparable, which is the point of having two ways to cut it.

Columns
Method. Country list, region and income group from the World Bank country endpoint, with the aggregate rows (“World”, “Euro area” and the rest) dropped, since they are sums of other rows and would be counted twice. Population, output per person and life expectancy are each that country’s most recent non-empty value, which for every row here is 2025; a country with no population figure is not on the page at all, which is why the count is 217 and not the 265 the endpoint returns. Read on 10 September 2026. What it cannot answer: anything inside a country. Every number here is a national aggregate, so a large unequal country and a small even one look the same from this height, and the page has no way to show you that.
