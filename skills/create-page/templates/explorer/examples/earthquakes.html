Three things the data says, one of them about itself

There are fewer small earthquakes than large ones, which is impossible.

667 in the magnitude 2 band against 923 in the 4 band. Every step up in magnitude should be roughly ten times rarer, so the shortfall is not geology, it is hearing: below about magnitude 4 the global network only catches what happens near a seismometer. The 4 band is the first one that is complete everywhere.

A third of the world’s earthquakes happened in Alaska.

805 of 2,223, more than Indonesia, Japan, the Philippines and Chile together. Alaska is seismically busy and it is also one of the most densely instrumented places on Earth, so this list is a map of seismometers as much as a map of earthquakes. The same effect puts California and Puerto Rico in the top five.

83% are in the top 70 km, and then there is a tail down to 629.

The deep ones are not scattered: they are slabs of old seafloor still sinking, which is why the deepest event of the month sits under Vanuatu at 629 km while the largest, a 7.8 near Ende in Indonesia, was only 10 km down. Turn the globe to the Pacific and the red dots line up along the trenches.

Source. Every row is real: the USGS magnitude 2.5+ past-month feed, fetched on 10 September 2026 and burned into this file as 167 KB of JSON — magnitude, latitude, longitude, depth, time, place, felt reports, tsunami flag and the USGS significance score, for 2,223 events between 11 August and 10 September. Nothing is sampled and nothing is rounded beyond three decimal places of position. The globe is drawn from that array with an orthographic projection and no mapping library; the flat map is Leaflet on OpenStreetMap tiles; the charts are Chart.js; the query box is SQLite compiled to WebAssembly. All four load from a CDN and all four fail softly — with the network off, the page still carries its numbers, its findings and this note. Counts of region come from the last comma-separated part of the USGS place string, which is a label rather than a country, so read them as where the USGS says it was.
