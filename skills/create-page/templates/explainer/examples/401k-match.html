Explainer

What a 401(k) match is, and how vesting works

Your offer letter says the company matches 100% of the first 3% you contribute and 50% of the next 2%, subject to a three-year cliff vesting schedule. That sentence decides how much free money you get and how much of it you keep if you leave, and nobody at orientation will explain it. Here is what each piece means, with the numbers worked through.

The short version

A 401(k) match is money your employer puts into your retirement account because you put money in, by a formula, up to a cap. It lands in your account beside your own contributions, but on a timer called vesting: you own your own money from day one, and you own the employer's money only after the years of service the plan sets, which the law caps at three years for a cliff and six for a graded schedule. Contribute at least enough to collect the whole match, and know your vesting date before you take another offer.

What it is

Two rules, one account

A match is a raise that only exists if you save. The plan says: for every dollar you put in, up to some percentage of your pay, the company adds some cents. Vesting answers a different question: if you leave, how much of the company's money goes with you? Your own contributions and their growth are always fully yours. The employer's contributions become yours on a schedule, and until they do, the plan takes them back when you go. The two rules are set separately, so a generous match with slow vesting can be worth less to a short-tenure employee than a smaller match owned immediately.

How it works

Four steps, with the offer letter's numbers

  1. 1

    You choose a percentage of each paycheck

    The plan takes it out before tax, or after tax if you choose Roth, and invests it in the funds you pick. The percentage is yours to change, usually at any time, and it is the only number in this story you control.

    Example. 5% of a $60,000 salary, paid twice a month $3,000 a year, $125 a paycheck
  2. 2

    The employer matches by the formula, paycheck by paycheck

    The formula has a rate and a cap. "100% of the first 3%" is dollar for dollar up to 3% of pay. "50% of the next 2%" is fifty cents on the dollar for the fourth and fifth percent. Above 5% of pay, you keep saving and the company adds nothing more.

    You contribute 3%: 100% of $1,800 $1,800 match
    You contribute 5%: $1,800 plus 50% of $1,200 $2,400 match, 4% of pay
    You contribute 10%: the cap was reached at 5% still $2,400
  3. 3

    The match lands in your account on a vesting clock

    The clock starts at your hire date and counts years of service, which most plans define as a year in which you worked at least 1,000 hours. The law allows two shapes. A cliff goes from 0% to 100% at once, no later than the third year. A graded schedule vests in steps, at least 20% a year starting in the second year, reaching 100% no later than the sixth.

    Years of service Your money Slowest cliff allowed Slowest graded allowed
    Under 2 100% 0% 0%
    2 100% 0% 20%
    3 100% 100% 40%
    4 100% 100% 60%
    5 100% 100% 80%
    6 100% 100% 100%

    These are the slowest schedules federal law allows for a matching contribution. A plan may vest faster, and some plan designs, called safe harbor, require the match to vest immediately. Yours is written in the Summary Plan Description.

    Example. After 2 years and 6 months, $6,000 of match has accumulated, before growth cliff: $0 yours · graded: 20%, $1,200 yours
  4. 4

    When you leave, the unvested part stays behind

    It is forfeited to the plan, which uses it to pay plan expenses or fund other people's match. Your own contributions, their growth, and the vested share of the employer's money roll over to an IRA or a new plan, or stay where they are. Under a cliff, the date you leave matters more than anything on the new offer.

    Example. Under the three-year cliff, leaving one month before the anniversary versus one month after forfeit the whole match · keep the whole match

What people get wrong

You might think
The match is a bonus I can spend.

In fact
It goes into the 401(k) beside your money and stays there under the same withdrawal rules. It is a raise you can only save.

You might think
The vesting clock starts when I enroll.

In fact
It starts at hire under most plans and counts service, not contributions. Someone who waited a year to enroll is still a year closer to vesting than a new hire.

You might think
If I put in a lot in January I collect the whole match early.

In fact
Most plans match each paycheck, so once your contributions stop for the year, the match stops too. Some plans add a true-up at year end; if yours does not, spread contributions across every paycheck.

You might think
Roth contributions get a Roth match.

In fact
The match usually goes in pre-tax whichever you chose, into a traditional bucket in the same account, and is taxed on the way out. A plan may offer a Roth match, but that is the plan's choice, not yours.

You might think
Leaving before the cliff forfeits the account.

In fact
Only the unvested employer money. Your contributions, their growth, and any vested share are yours to roll over.

Why it matters to you

  • The formula sets your floor. Below 5% of pay, you are declining part of a raise.
  • Your hire date is a financial date. Under a cliff, a month early can cost the whole match.
  • Per-paycheck matching punishes front-loading unless the plan trues up at year end.
