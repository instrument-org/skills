Recommendation with criteria

A database for a small B2B SaaS

4 options · 7 criteria · scale 1 to 5 · weights sum to 100

Postgres, MySQL, MongoDB, and SQLite with Litestream, for a two-engineer team shipping a multi-tenant product. Postgres wins at 87 of 100, twelve points clear of MySQL. Two of the three alternatives cannot win under any weighting of these criteria; the third wins only when operational burden is nearly half the score.

Result

The goal

A primary database for a B2B product with organizations, users, invoices, and an audit log, where every query is scoped to a tenant. Forty customers today, a plan for four hundred in three years, one region, two engineers who also carry the pager, and under $100 a month for the database at launch. The choice has to survive the first hire who did not make it.

Criteria and weights

25 Relational fit Tenants, invoices, and an audit log are joins and constraints. The heaviest weight because a bad fit here is paid on every feature.
20 Operational burden Two engineers, no ops hire planned. Upgrades, vacuum, connection limits, and 3 a.m. pages all count.
15 Managed hosting How many credible hosts run it, and what the smallest production tier costs.
15 Ecosystem ORM support, migration tooling, and how often the team will be the first to hit a bug.
10 Headroom to 10x Four hundred tenants is still small. Weighted below burden on purpose; the plan is to grow, not to shard.
10 Backup and recovery Point-in-time recovery that has been rehearsed, not a nightly dump that has not.
5 Team familiarity Both engineers have shipped on Postgres. Lightest, because a week of learning is cheaper than a year of fighting.

  • Relational fit. Postgres has row-level security for tenant scoping and JSONB for the odd flexible column. MongoDB gets two dots because tenant joins and invoice constraints are the app's whole shape.
  • Operational burden. Every server database costs three dots: upgrades, connection pooling, and one vacuum surprise a year. SQLite with Litestream is one process and one bucket, so five.
  • Managed hosting. Postgres has the most hosts and the cheapest production tier at about $20 a month; SQLite has none, which is the point and the risk.
  • Headroom. SQLite writes from one process. Fine at forty tenants, a rewrite at four hundred if the write path grows.

Why Postgres, 87 of 100

It scores best or tied on every criterion except operational burden, where all three server databases tie and only SQLite pulls ahead. Its lead is built from the heaviest criteria: relational fit and ecosystem alone give it eight of its twelve-point margin over MySQL, and the cheapest managed tier on the list keeps it under the $100 launch budget with room for a replica.

Why not the runner-up

MySQL is a sound database and a sound second. It loses twelve points to Postgres in four places: one dot on relational fit (no row-level security, weaker JSON and constraints), one on managed hosting (fewer hosts aimed at small teams, and the cheap tiers are Postgres-first), one on ecosystem (migration tools and ORMs default to Postgres and test there first), and one on familiarity. It scores at or below Postgres on every row, so no weighting of these seven criteria makes it win; choosing it would mean adding a criterion, such as an existing MySQL estate to match, that this team does not have.

Weighting for the four-hundred-tenant plan widens the lead: Postgres 89, MySQL 77, MongoDB 62, SQLite 62. SQLite falls to a tie for last.
Never wins
MySQL and MongoDB both score at or below Postgres on every criterion, so neither can win under any weighting of these seven. MongoDB would need a criterion this app does not have, such as schemaless documents at high write volume.
