Recommendation guide

A CRM for a four-person consulting shop

Eighty leads a year, a dozen active clients, a spreadsheet that has stopped scaling, a hard cap of $50 per seat per month, and a firm that lives in Google Workspace. Move to Copper: it is the only serious option on the list that treats Gmail and Calendar as the system of record rather than a plugin.

Our pick

Copper, Starter plan

$29 per seat per month, billed annually · four seats, about $1,400 a year

Copper was built inside Google Workspace: every lead, email thread, and meeting lands in the CRM without anyone remembering to log it, which is the exact failure that broke the spreadsheet. At 80 leads a year, the pipeline features of bigger tools would sit unused.

Who this is for

  • Partner-led firms where each person owns their own relationships and nobody wants to be the CRM administrator.
  • Teams whose deals close in email and meetings, not in a web form.

Stop reading if

  • You run outbound sequences or inbound marketing at volume. You want HubSpot, and you will outgrow the free tier fast.
  • Anyone on the team is on Outlook. Copper's edge disappears and Pipedrive becomes the pick.

    Top pick

    Wins when the firm lives in Gmail and Calendar and wants the CRM to fill itself in. Contact enrichment, a shared pipeline, and a Chrome sidebar that shows the deal beside the thread.

    Wins when the pipeline board matters more than the inbox. Cleaner deal stages and better reporting than Copper, but Gmail sync is a two-way add-on you have to keep an eye on.

    Budget

    Streak $0 for basic, $49 per seat per month for Pro

    Wins when the firm refuses to leave Gmail at all: the pipeline is a set of columns inside the inbox. Free tier covers two people comfortably; the Pro tier is at your cap and still has no shared reporting worth the name.

  4. 4

    Upgrade

    Copper, Professional $69 per seat per month, over the cap

    Wins when the firm grows past eight people or starts needing workflow automation and multiple pipelines. Same product, so the upgrade is a checkbox rather than a migration.

Flaws, not dealbreakers

  • Starter caps you at 2,500 contacts. Fine for years at 80 leads a year, but importing an old mailing list will hit it in an afternoon; keep the list out of the CRM.
  • Reporting on Starter is a handful of fixed dashboards. If a partner wants to slice pipeline by service line, export to Sheets.
  • The annual price is the advertised one; month to month runs about 20 percent higher, which pushes it to $35.
  • Auto-logging captures every thread with a contact, including personal ones with a client you also know socially. Each person should review the exclusion rules in the first week.

What mattered, in order

  • Zero-effort logging decisiveThe spreadsheet failed because logging was a chore. A CRM that depends on discipline fails the same way.
  • Google Workspace fit decisiveContacts, Calendar, and Drive links must show up without a sync job to babysit.
  • Price under $50 importantPer seat, per month, on the plan that actually has the features above. Free tiers that lose those features were scored on the paid tier.
  • No administrator importantFour consultants, no ops person. Setup has to be an afternoon and stay set up.
  • Pipeline reporting niceUseful for the quarterly partner meeting, not for the daily work. Weighted last on purpose.

How we chose

Seven CRMs were shortlisted from the ones that list a Google Workspace integration. Each was scored on the paid tier under $50 against the five criteria above, with the two decisive criteria weighted double. Copper and Pipedrive were the only two to clear both decisive criteria; Copper cleared them without an add-on. Nothing was trialed with the firm's own data; the scoring is from the vendors' documentation and current reviews, so the first week of a Copper trial is the real test.

What we skipped

  • HubSpot Starter. Best free tier on the list, but the plan with real pipeline features lands above $50 per seat once you add the sales hub.
  • Zoho CRM. Under budget and capable, but the Gmail integration is an add-on with its own settings, and the interface assumes an administrator.
  • Folk. Lovely for personal networks, but four people sharing one pipeline is where it thins out.
  • Attio. The strongest product on the list, and the wrong shape: built for teams modeling their data, which is a hobby this firm does not have.
  • Notion as a CRM. The spreadsheet again, with nicer views and the same logging problem.
