Should I refinance my 30-year mortgage now?

You are at 6.9 percent, lenders are quoting about 6.1, and you expect to stay about five more years. That is a 0.8 point spread: enough to pay off, not enough to pay off in every shape, and which shape wins for you comes down to what you pay to close and how firm that five years is.

Short answer

It depends

On how long you really stay, and on whether you pay the closing costs or let the lender bury them in the rate.

On a $385,000 balance, 6.1 percent cuts the payment by about $258 a month and pays back roughly $7,500 in closing costs by month 29. Stay five years and you finish about $8,000 ahead. But a lender-paid refinance at about 6.5 percent, with nothing at closing, finishes the same five years about $9,400 ahead, and the paid version only overtakes it in year seven.

What makes it a clear yes: a real Loan Estimate at 6.1 with zero points and under $8,000 to close, and a decade in the house. What makes it a no: any real chance you sell before 2029 and a quote that carries a point.

What you told us

  • A 30-year fixed at 6.9 percent, two years in
  • Quotes around 6.1 percent for a new 30-year
  • About five more years in the house

What the math assumes

  • $385,000 left with 28 years to go: $2,591 a month in principal and interest
  • Closing costs of $7,500, about 2 percent of the loan
  • A lender-paid alternative priced at 6.5 percent

What it depends on

  1. 01

    How firm "five years" is. The paid refinance breaks even at month 29 and overtakes the lender-paid one at month 74. Under 29 months, do nothing that costs money; past 74, pay the costs and take the lower rate.

  2. 02

    What you actually pay to close. $7,500 is the assumption. Every $1,000 more pushes break-even out four months and the crossover ten. Above $10,000, the five-year case for paying is gone.

  3. 03

    Whether the 6.1 carries points. One discount point is $3,850, and it is often how a 6.1 gets quoted when the zero-point rate is 6.3. Ask for the zero-point rate by name.

  4. 04

    What the lender-paid rate is. At 6.5 percent it saves $157 a month with no break-even to clear. At 6.6 the two shapes tie at exactly five years; at 6.7 the paid one wins from year four.

  5. 05

    Whether you want the payment or the payoff date. About $60 of the $258 comes from restarting the clock at 30 years, not from the rate. Judged at your current payoff date the saving is $198, and the deal has to work on that number.

By how long you stay

If you stay 3 years

Not this deal

Paying $7,500 to close nets $1,800 at best and loses money at your current payoff date, and then you pay a second set of closing costs to sell. If you refinance at all, take the lender-paid rate: about $5,600 ahead with nothing at risk.

If you stay 5 years

Yes, shop the shape

Both versions pay. The lender-paid one pays more, about $9,400 to $8,000, and risks nothing if five turns into three. Pay the closing costs only if the zero-point 6.1 is real and you would bet on staying past 2032.

If you stay 10 years

Yes, pay to close

The 6.1 finishes about $23,000 ahead against $19,000 for the lender-paid rate, and even a quote with a point baked in pays back by year four. Do it, and ask to amortize over 28 years so the clock does not restart.

What goes wrong

  • The point you did not see. A 6.1 that carries one point costs $3,850 more at closing and moves break-even to month 44. The five-year case is gone and you will not know until the Loan Estimate.
  • A low appraisal. Under about $481,000 you cross 80 percent loan-to-value and pick up mortgage insurance, which eats most of the $258 for years.
  • The reset that flatters the number. Two extra years of payments make the monthly saving look bigger than the rate earned. If the deal only works at $258 and not at $198, it does not work.
  • A lock that expires. A 45-day rate lock in a slow closing costs about a quarter point to extend, or the rate you locked.

Next step

Get three Loan Estimates on the same day, each priced both ways

Ask each lender for the zero-point rate with costs and for the lender-paid rate with none. It costs an hour and one credit pull; every mortgage pull inside 45 days counts as one. Read the rate on page 1 and line J, Total Closing Costs, on page 2. If the best paid quote is 6.1 with under $8,000 to close and you would bet on seven years, sign it. Otherwise sign the lender-paid one, or keep 6.9 and set a reminder for six months.

Questions people ask

Should I take a 15-year instead?
At about 5.6 percent the payment is $3,170, roughly $580 more a month than now, and the house is paid off in 2041. Only if that payment leaves the budget calm.
Do I have to restart at 30 years?
No. Ask for a 25-year, or to amortize to your current payoff date. The monthly saving drops to $198 and the interest saving grows.
Will it hurt my credit?
A few points for a few months. Mortgage inquiries inside a 45-day window count as one, so shopping three lenders costs the same as shopping one.
Should I wait for rates to fall further?
Nobody times it. If they fall to 5.5 next year you can refinance again, and the lender-paid shape makes that free; if they rise, you kept 6.9. Decide on today's spread.
