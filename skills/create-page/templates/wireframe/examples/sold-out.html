When it sells out while you are typing your card in

Today a checkout that loses its hold shows a red box, empties the form and sends you back to the event page, where the button now says Sold out. It is the single worst thirty seconds we ship, and it happens most to the people who were quickest. These five frames keep the form, name what actually happened, and turn the dead end into the only two things that are still true: a waitlist with a real hold window, and the other dates.

, in the order they happen

What the frames settle, and what they do not

Settled by the frames

  • The form survives. Frame 3 keeps every field the person filled in, including the card, because retyping it is the part they will tell people about.
  • The failure is explained in the place it happened. No redirect back to the event page. The person stays on the screen they were on, which is where their attention already is.
  • The waitlist promises a number, not a feeling. Frame 4 commits to a ten-minute hold and a position. “We’ll let you know” is what we have and it is why nobody joins.
  • Other dates are an offer, not a consolation. They sit beside the waitlist at equal weight, because for half of these people a different night is the better answer.

Still open

  • Whether we can hold a card without charging it. Frame 3 assumes the entered details are still in the session and re-submittable. If they are tokenized and one-shot, frame 4 needs a re-entry step and the argument gets weaker.
  • Ten minutes, at 11pm. Drawn as ten because that is what fits on the screen. Whether it is the right number at the hour releases actually happen is a question for whoever has the data.
  • Position in queue. Shown as “4th”. If the queue is not actually ordered, do not draw a number; an honest “we don’t promise an order” beats an invented one.
  • Partial availability. These frames assume four tickets or none. Two of four is the harder case and is not drawn.

Reading these

Frames 1 to 4 are drawn at 1280×800, a laptop browser. Frame 5 is 480×320, because a release notice is a small surface and drawing it the size of a window would misrepresent it. Click any frame to open it.

  • The banner in frame 3, the waitlist copy in frame 4 and the release notice in frame 5 are final-quality. The event description, the venue blurb and the footer are grey bars, and staying out of them is the point of drawing them that way.
  • The venue, the artist, the dates and the prices are invented. They exist so the rows have something the right length in them. Nothing here is a claim about any real event or any real price.
  • Drawn against nothing. No current screen was measured. The browser chrome is generic so the conversation stays on the flow.
  • Not drawn: the queue page people wait in before checkout opens, the accessible-seating path, and anything about refunds. The first of those probably deserves its own set.
A proposal, not a record. No existing screen was measured for these, and the venue, the performer, the dates, the prices and the one-in-twelve return rate are all invented to give the frames something the right shape to hold. What the drawing argues is the sequence and the copy; the return rate in frame 4 is exactly the kind of number that has to come from someone’s data before it is printed on a page.
