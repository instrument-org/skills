TLDR brief · E-bike battery rules for a US bike shop

Sell only certified bikes and packs, charge nothing overnight, and never rebuild a battery. The law is catching up to what your insurer already requires.

For the owner of a US shop that sells, services, and stores e-bikes. Two minutes. Rules are as of this week; the long notes are linked at the end.

What the marks mean

UL 2849 certifies the whole electrical system of a bike: pack, charger, motor, and wiring together. UL 2271 certifies a pack on its own. Why it matters: a listing that says "UL charger" says nothing about the pack, and a certified pack on an uncertified bike is not a certified bike.

Where it is law

New York City has banned the sale, lease, and rental of uncertified e-bikes and packs since September 2023. California requires UL 2849 or EN 15194 on bikes sold from January 2026 and UL 2271 on standalone packs from 2027. About a dozen other states have bills. Outside all of them, a fire in an uncertified bike you sold is a claim you lose.

What's next, federally

The Consumer Product Safety Commission has no mandatory standard yet; it has sent letters to makers, run recalls, and opened a rulemaking. Expect a national rule that looks like UL 2849 within two or three years, and expect it to grandfather nothing on your floor.

Your insurer moves first

Carriers are adding exclusions for uncertified batteries and for any pack a shop has opened. Why it matters: the exclusion arrives at renewal with no press release. Ask your agent for the endorsement language before you sign.

On the shop floor

Store packs at 30 to 60 percent charge, charge on a noncombustible surface during open hours with someone present, keep charging away from exits, and put damaged packs in a lithium-rated cabinet or bag outside the building. These are the fire department's rules, and the ones an adjuster checks first.

Shipping a pack back

A lithium pack ships as class 9 hazmat, UN 3480 on its own or UN 3481 inside a bike, which needs a trained shipper, the maker's UN 38.3 test summary, and a carrier that accepts it. Why it matters: returning a warranty pack is the part of a claim that stalls.

The catch

Opening a pack ends its certification. Re-celling, swapping a battery management board, or fitting a "compatible" aftermarket pack to a certified bike moves the liability from the maker to you, and voids the very listing your insurer and your city require. Decline the job, every time, and say why.

Do

  • Ask suppliers for the certificate number and look it up in UL's product directory before the first order.
  • Write "certified only" into the purchasing policy and the consignment agreement.
  • Post the charging rules at the bench and train every hire on them in week one.
  • Point customers with uncertified bikes at the trade-in programs; several cities pay for the swap.

Do not

  • Take an uncertified bike on consignment, or service one you would not sell.
  • Leave customer bikes charging after close, even on a timer.
  • Store a swollen or crash-damaged pack indoors overnight.
  • Open a pack, for any reason, including a customer who offers to sign a waiver.

Go deeper

  • UL Product iQ, to check a certificate number against the maker's claim.
  • CPSC, for recalls by brand and the state of the federal rulemaking.
  • FDNY, for the storage and charging guidance most other cities copy.
  • NFPA, for the lithium battery safety sheets to post at the bench.
