Briefing memo

Piloting a four-day week for one quarter

To
Priya Raman, CEO
From
Tom Okafor, Head of People
Date
September 8, 2026
Re
A one-quarter pilot of a four-day week for the 25-person team

Bottom line up front

Run the pilot in Q1 2027: 32 hours at full pay, Fridays off for the whole company, a two-person rotation covering support on Fridays, and a written go/no-go on April 17 against three measures we set now. The cash cost is under $10,000. The real cost is delivery risk, and the real danger is a pilot with no criteria that drifts into a permanent change nobody chose. Decision needed by October 15, 2026.

Key facts

  • 25 people: 14 engineering and product, 5 support and success, 3 sales, 3 finance and operations.
  • Support covers 8 to 6 Pacific on weekdays. Friday carries 17 percent of tickets, the lightest day.
  • Velocity: 61 points a sprint on average over four quarters, standard deviation 9.
  • Attrition: 5 of 25 in twelve months; replacing an engineer costs about $45,000 in recruiting and ramp.
  • 17 of 25 want it, per the June survey. eNPS was 24.
  • Pay unchanged: 32 hours at 100 percent. Cash cost under $10,000 for the quarter.
  • Window: January 5 to April 3, 2027, clear of peak and annual planning.

Recommendation

Option A: Fridays off, 32 hours, full pay

Thirteen weeks from January 5, with a two-person Friday support rotation. Go or no-go on April 17 against velocity, Friday response time, and eNPS.

Decide by October 15, 2026

1.Context

The team asked. In the June engagement survey, 17 of 25 people said they would want a four-day week, and three of the five people who left in the last year named burnout or flexibility in their exit interview. Two of the four companies we lose engineering candidates to advertise a four-day week. We are 25 people at $4.2 million of annual recurring revenue, with customers who expect support from 8 to 6 Pacific on weekdays. Doing nothing is a choice too: the survey result is known to everyone who answered it, and a year of silence will read as an answer.

2.Options

Option A: Fridays off, whole company, 32 hoursRecommended

Everyone takes Friday. Support runs two people each Friday, so each of the five works two Fridays in five, and sales keeps Friday for prospects by choice and takes Monday instead. It is the version the team asked for and the version the published trials measured.

Cost
Under $10,000 cash: the two hourly staff at 32 hours on the same rate, and a Friday stipend for support. Plan for velocity within 10 percent.
Risk
Friday first response slows. A visible benefit that is hard to take back if the measures come in mixed.
Forecloses
Friday customer meetings by default for a quarter, and the quieter staggered trial below.

Option B: A staggered day off, Monday or Friday, 32 hours

Each person picks Monday or Friday and teams keep three shared days. Coverage is easier and nothing about the company's week changes from the outside. What it gives up is the shared day, which the published trials say is where most of the morale effect comes from.

Cost
Same cash. Two thin days to schedule around instead of one.
Risk
Monday and Friday both become half-days for meetings, and the pilot measures a weaker version of the idea.
Forecloses
Little. The easiest option to end quietly.

Option C: A compressed week, four nine-hour days

Fridays off at 36 hours, so the same work in longer days. The evidence is that compressed weeks move burnout rather than reduce it, and the team asked for less work, not a rearranged schedule. Offered as a pilot, it will read as the offer they did not make.

Cost
None in hours or cash.
Risk
Longer days for parents and commuters; a survey result answered with something else.
Forecloses
The credibility of a real trial later.

3.Recommendation

Option A. It is the version the team asked for, the version the trials measured, and it has a clean end: three measures, one date. The support rotation is the load-bearing detail; if we cannot staff two people every Friday, we should not start. The measures, set now: sprint velocity within 10 percent of the trailing four-quarter average of 61 points; Friday first response under two hours; eNPS in the March survey above June's 24. Two of three carries it forward. Fewer ends it on April 3, with thanks and the numbers.

  1. Friday support rotation and coverage plan. Sam Ortiz, support, by October 1.
  2. Velocity and response-time baselines pulled and frozen. Tom with engineering, by October 15.
  3. Customer note for the January release notes. Tom, by December 1.
  4. Decision at the October 15 leadership meeting.

4.Open questions

  1. Do the three salespeople keep Fridays? Prospects work Fridays, and the proposal is that sales takes Monday. Ask them rather than decide for them. Tom, by September 26.
  2. What do we tell customers? A line in the January release notes, or nothing. Nothing risks a customer learning about it from a slow Friday. Priya, with Sam.
  3. What about contractors and the two hourly staff? Hourly at 32 hours on the same rate is a raise of about $6,000 for the quarter; contractors are on their own terms. Finance, by October 1.
  4. What if the measures come in mixed, say velocity down 12 percent and eNPS up 20 points? The go/no-go has to say now which measure wins. Priya and Tom, before October 15.
  5. Does PTO accrual change on a four-day week? Recommend no change for the pilot, and a decision only if it becomes permanent. Tom, in the pilot note.
