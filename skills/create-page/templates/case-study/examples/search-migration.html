Case study / engineering

Moving a Rails app's search from Elasticsearch to Postgres full-text search

Ledgerline is a B2B invoicing product with about nine million invoices. Its search ran on a managed Elasticsearch cluster that was one engineer's part-time job to keep in sync. Over eight weeks a team of five replaced it with a generated tsvector column and a GIN index, and turned the cluster off.

stack
Rails 7.1, Postgres 15, Sidekiq
team
5 engineers
duration
8 weeks
rows
9.1M invoices, 31M line items, 410k customers

01 · Context

Search in Ledgerline is one box in the header. Customers type an invoice number, a customer name, or an email and expect the matching invoice on the first line. The original implementation, from four years earlier, indexed invoices, customers, and line items into Elasticsearch 7 on a managed three-node cluster costing about $1,100 a month. A Sidekiq job enqueued on every after_commit kept the index current, and the app queried the cluster for IDs, then loaded rows from Postgres.

The team was five engineers with no dedicated operations role. The database was a single Postgres 15 primary with one read replica, on a managed host, with headroom.

02 · Challenge

Three pressures arrived together. Elasticsearch 7 was approaching the end of the host's support window, and the upgrade to 8 meant a full reindex, which had last taken about six hours with writes paused to the sync worker. The sync itself was the source of a recurring bug: when the queue backed up, voided invoices stayed visible in search for minutes, and one customer had filed it three times. And the cluster was a second system to be paged for. In the six months before the project, four of the team's pages were search-related: a queue backlog after a deploy, a mapping conflict from a renamed field, a node at yellow, and a disk alert.

Nobody on the team had operated Elasticsearch by choice. The question was whether Postgres could carry the query load without making search worse, and nobody knew, because nobody had looked at what people actually searched for.

03 · Approach, in order

  1. week 1
    Audited the queries. One week of search logs, about 140,000 searches, classified by shape. 94 percent were an exact or prefix match on an invoice number, a customer name, or an email. Six percent were free text that could only be matching line item descriptions. The faceted filters in the search UI had been clicked 31 times in the week. The audit settled in an afternoon an argument the team had been having for a quarter.
  2. weeks 2 to 3
    Prototyped on a replica snapshot. A generated tsvector column on invoices, weighted so the number and customer name outrank the memo, with a GIN index built concurrently. Line items got their own column and index, queried through an EXISTS subquery for the six percent. Invoice number prefixes were handled by appending :* to the last term when the input matched the number format.
  3. weeks 4 to 5
    Ran both in shadow. Every production search hit Elasticsearch for the response and Postgres in the background, and the job logged the overlap of the two top-ten lists. Overlap was 97 percent across the two weeks. The three percent were mostly multi-word customer names, which Postgres ranked lower than a partial match on the invoice memo; adding a phrase-match boost from phraseto_tsquery closed most of the gap.
  4. week 6
    Cut over behind a flag per account. Ten percent of accounts for five days, then all. One regression came in on day two: searching for the local part of an email returned nothing, because the simple parser treats an address as one token. The fix was a pg_trgm index on customer_email and a second clause in the scope. The same week, autovacuum fell behind on the invoices table and the GIN index bloated; raising the table's vacuum scale factor and running one manual vacuum took a Saturday.
  5. weeks 7 to 8
    Deleted the old path. The sync worker, the mapping files, the client wrapper, and their tests: about 1,400 lines net, by the PR diff. The primary was moved up one tier for headroom. The cluster was turned off at the end of week 8, one week after the flag reached 100 percent.

06 · What we would do differently

  • Build a labeled relevance set before the shadow run. Fifty real queries with the expected first result would have turned overlap into a quality measure. Overlap only proved the two systems agreed.
  • Shadow through a month-end. Search volume triples in the last three days of a month, and the two-week window missed one. It went fine, but that was luck.
  • Index emails as trigrams from day one. The parser treats an address as one token; the regression was predictable and cost a customer-facing day.
  • Tune autovacuum in the same PR as the GIN index. A large table gaining a GIN index changes its vacuum needs. The Saturday was avoidable.
  • Keep the cluster on for a month, not a week. It was not needed, but a month of $1,100 was cheap insurance against being wrong about the six percent.

What held up: doing the query audit first, the generated column over a trigger, the flag per account, and deleting the old path in one PR so nobody could quietly keep it alive.

Apply this

This transfers when

  • Most searches are exact or prefix matches on a few known fields. Check the logs, not the UI.
  • The corpus lives in one Postgres primary and is in the tens of millions of rows, not billions.
  • Nobody uses facets, aggregations, or typo tolerance, whatever the UI offers.
  • You can run both paths in shadow and log the difference.

It does not when

  • Faceted navigation or aggregations are the product, as in a catalog or an analytics view.
  • You need fuzzy matching across many languages, or ranking tuned by a search team.
  • The searchable data is spread across several databases or services.
  • The primary has no headroom; the column and index cost real disk and vacuum time.

First step. Pull one week of search logs and classify each query by shape. If fewer than 80 percent are structured matches on known fields, this case does not apply, and you have learned that in an afternoon.
