Case study

How a 12-person design studio moved to a four-day week, and what the first year showed

Wren & Field, a brand and product design studio in Denver, closed on Fridays in January and kept pay whole. A year later revenue was level, nobody had left, and the founders had learned that the week was a pricing decision before it was a scheduling one.

01Context

Wren & Field was founded in 2016 by two designers and had grown to twelve: the two founders, seven designers, a strategist, a producer who ran every schedule, and an operations lead who ran everything else. Revenue the year before the change was $2.1 million, from six monthly retainers and about twenty projects a year across fourteen active clients. Most work was brand systems and product design for funded startups and two regional consumer brands.

The studio billed a mix: retainers were priced as a block of hours a month, and roughly half of project work was estimated and invoiced hourly. That detail mattered more than anyone expected.

02Challenge

In the eighteen months before the trial, three designers left, two of them for in-house roles that paid about the same and promised better hours. Replacing each took about four months and pulled a founder off client work for most of it. The founders wanted a retention edge that did not mean raising pay across the board, which the margin could not carry.

Three things stood in the way. Clients expected a reply the same day, five days a week, and two of the retainers were with marketing teams that shipped campaigns on Fridays. Hourly billing meant that a 20 percent shorter week was, on paper, a 20 percent pay cut for the studio. And nobody knew where the current forty hours went, so nobody could say which eight could go.

03Approach, in the order it happened

  1. October to November

    Measured first. Everyone tracked time for eight weeks in the tool the studio already invoiced from. Internal meetings, status updates, and time spent on estimates that never became work averaged eleven hours per person per week. That was the number the trial was paid for with.

  2. December

    Announced a six-month trial starting the second week of January: 32 hours, Fridays off for the whole studio, pay unchanged, with three written measures for a go or no-go in July: revenue against the prior year, milestones delivered on time, and departures. The calendar was rewritten in the same week. The Monday all-hands hour went; daily standups became a written note; each client account got one thirty-minute check-in a week instead of one per project.

  3. December

    Repriced the work. Every new project from January was quoted as a fixed fee. Retainers were restated at renewal as deliverables per month rather than hours per month. Two legacy hourly clients were left as they were until their contracts ended in the spring, and the founders accepted the smaller invoices on those two accounts for one quarter.

  4. January

    Set up Friday coverage. One person a week took the Friday rotation: check every client channel at ten and at three, spend no more than thirty minutes each time, escalate to a founder only for a live production problem, and take a half day back the following week. Each of the fourteen clients got a two-paragraph email the week before. Two asked questions. None asked for a discount, and none left during the year.

  5. March

    Hit the first setback. A website launch slipped by a week because its kickoff landed on a Thursday before a Friday off and a Monday holiday, and the client's feedback sat for four days. The producer added two rules: no kickoffs on Thursdays, and launch windows on Tuesdays and Wednesdays only. Milestone dates began to be logged formally that month, which is why there is no clean before figure for delivery.

  6. July

    Made it permanent. Revenue for the half year was slightly ahead of the prior year's, no milestone had been missed since March, and nobody had left. The founders wrote the four-day week into offer letters and posted the first open role with it in the title.

04What the figures leave out

Billable hours per person fell, from about 26 a week to about 24 by the studio's own tracking, which the founders expected; fixed fees are what kept revenue level while hours fell. The client count went from fourteen to fifteen. Delivery improved by the producer's account, but the studio only began logging milestone dates in March, so the year before exists as memory rather than a record, and this page does not turn that memory into a percentage.

Two things did not go as hoped. The two hourly legacy accounts invoiced about a fifth less for the quarter before they were repriced, which the founders absorbed. And the Friday rotation was unpopular with the two most junior designers, who felt the half day back did not match the interruption; the studio changed it in the autumn so a founder always took the rotation in a week with a launch.

05Lessons

  1. 1Measure before you change anything. Eight weeks of tracking found the eleven hours a week the four-day week was paid for with. Without that figure the trial would have been an act of faith.
  2. 2A shorter week is a pricing decision. On hourly billing it is a pay cut for the business. Reprice first, or accept a quarter of smaller invoices on the accounts you cannot.
  3. 3Coverage beats availability. One person checking twice for thirty minutes was enough. Nobody needed to be reachable all Friday, and clients adjusted within a month.
  4. 4Tell clients once, plainly, before they notice. Two questions out of fourteen, and no departures. The fear of the email was larger than the email.
  5. 5Write the go or no-go before day one. Three measures on a page kept a trial from drifting into a habit nobody chose, and made July a decision rather than a mood.
  6. 6Kickoffs and launches are the fragile days. Put them mid-week. The one slipped project of the year came from a Thursday kickoff.

Apply this

This transfers when

  • You bill fixed fees or retainers, or can move to them within a quarter.
  • Client contact is weekly by nature: reviews, check-ins, milestones, not a live queue.
  • You are small enough to rewrite the calendar in a week and to know where the hours go.

It does not when

  • You sell hours and cannot reprice: staffing, hourly consulting, most legal work.
  • Clients need same-day action five days a week, as with live campaigns or support contracts.
  • You cannot absorb one quarter of smaller invoices while pricing catches up.

First step. Track time for eight weeks, in whatever tool you already invoice from, and count the hours no client asked for. If it is under six a week, the four-day week will come out of client work, and this case does not apply.
