Case study

The bakery that stopped taking Thanksgiving on paper

Marigold Bakehouse sold pies for two holiday weeks a year out of one notebook and a dry-erase board. Then the owner put a single ordering page on the counter, cut the menu, took the money up front, and got her November back.

A neighborhood bakery · six people · two holiday rounds

The shop

Marigold Bakehouse has been on the same corner since 2014. Six people work there: the owner, who also does the books and the counter on weekends; a head baker; two bakers who start at four; and two counter staff, one part-time. The daily trade is bread, morning pastries, and cakes to order, and it runs on habit and a good espresso machine.

Twice a year the shop becomes a different business. In the week before Thanksgiving it bakes pies almost exclusively, and in the two weeks before Christmas it bakes pies, stollen, and cookie boxes. Those three weeks are the best of the year for takings and the worst for everyone's sleep. Two deck ovens can turn out about 140 pies a day if the regular bread is cut back to almost nothing, and the shop had never once planned to that number.

The problem with the book

Holiday orders came in three ways: written into a paper book by the register, taken over the phone and written into the same book by whoever answered, or added by the owner from messages on the shop's social accounts. A dry-erase board in the back tracked the count per day, updated when someone remembered. Deposits were taken in cash, sometimes.

The failures were the same every year. Twice the year before, a day's count had crept past what the ovens could bake and the bakers stayed until midnight. About thirty pies went unclaimed on the Wednesday before Thanksgiving, prepaid by nobody, and were given away or thrown out. In the final week the counter staff spent most of each morning on the phone, and on Wednesday the pickup line reached the door and stayed there.

The owner had looked at building a shop website twice and stopped both times. She did not want to sell bread online, did not want to learn a platform, and did not want to give a percentage of a pie to anyone. What she wanted was narrower: for the two weeks to stop depending on one notebook.

What they did, in order

September. The point-of-sale system the shop already used offered a pre-order page as an add-on. The owner turned it on one afternoon with the part-time counter person, who was the least patient with software in the building and therefore the right tester. They agreed that if it took more than an afternoon to understand, they would turn it off.

October. The holiday menu went from sixteen items to nine. Everything that needed a conversation, a custom size, or a second oven setting was cut. Each remaining item got a daily cap, and the daily caps added up to what the ovens could do: 120 pies for the Tuesday, 140 for the Wednesday, none on the holiday itself, when the shop is closed. Orders would be prepaid in full, with pickup in thirty-minute windows of no more than 24 orders each.

November 1. Orders opened with a sign on the counter, a line on the bag stamp, and one post. The phone stayed on for regulars, with one rule: every phone order was entered into the same page by whoever took it, so the counts were true. The head baker started printing a pick list by window each morning, and a folding table went by the door for pickups, with bags labeled by name and window.

The week of. Wednesday's windows sold out four days before the holiday, which had never happened. The week also showed what was wrong. People arrived at the start of each window, so twice the line still reached the door. The page did not say a day was sold out, so people phoned to ask. And three orders went home with the wrong pie because two customers shared a surname.

The Monday after. The owner, the head baker, and the counter staff sat down for an hour. Windows went to twenty minutes with sixteen orders each. A sold-out day would show as sold out. Labels would carry a first name and the last four digits of a phone number. Christmas orders opened December 1 with those three changes and nothing else, and the round ran with the same six people and no extra hours on the phone.

Before

  • Orders in a paper book, by phone, and in messages.
  • Counts on a dry-erase board, updated when remembered.
  • Sixteen holiday items, several needing a conversation.
  • Cash deposits, sometimes. About thirty pies unclaimed.
  • Everyone picks up on Wednesday afternoon.

After

  • One ordering page; phone orders typed into the same list.
  • Daily caps set at what the ovens can bake.
  • Nine items, none needing a phone call.
  • Prepaid in full. Fewer than ten pies unclaimed.
  • Twenty-minute pickup windows and a pick list by window.

How it went

The shop sold out of Thanksgiving pies four days early for the first time anyone there could remember, and sold out of Christmas pies a week early. Unclaimed pies went from about thirty to fewer than ten across both rounds, which the owner puts down entirely to prepayment. The ovens were never overbooked, because the caps would not let them be. The pickup line moved, once the windows were shortened.

Holiday-week takings were higher than the year before, but the owner is careful about what that means: the caps were set at what the ovens could do, so the ceiling had not moved. What changed was baking to a paid list instead of a guess, and not throwing thirty pies away. Phone time in the final week fell to a few calls a day by her estimate; nobody measured it before, so there is no figure to compare. A few older regulars still phoned, and always will, and the counter staff typed their orders in for them.

Two seasons at one shop is thin evidence, and holiday demand was strong that year everywhere. The claim this story supports is narrower and firmer: a small shop can move its peak onto a single list without building a store, and the parts that matter are the caps, the prepayment, and the menu, not the software.

What they would tell another shop

  • Cut the menu before you cap the orders. Nine items made everything else possible.
  • Set the caps at what the ovens can do, not at what you sold last year.
  • Take the money up front. Unclaimed pies were a deposit problem, not a customer problem.
  • Keep the phone, but keep one list. The counts are only true if every order lands in the same place.
  • Short windows beat long ones. People arrive at the start of whatever window you give them.
  • Run the first round on the smaller holiday if you have one. They did not, and every setback landed on the biggest week of the year.

Apply this

This transfers when

  • Your peak is short and predictable: holidays, graduations, game days.
  • Your capacity is a hard number you can write down: ovens, hours, seats, hands.
  • You are willing to cut the peak menu to what needs no conversation.

It does not when

  • The peak is every day. A cafe queue is a staffing problem, not a pre-order one.
  • Orders need a conversation: wedding cakes, custom work, anything sized on the phone.
  • Your customers will not prepay, as with wholesale and invoiced accounts.

First step. Write down two numbers: how many things you can make on your busiest day, and how many items are on the peak menu. If the second is over ten, cut it before you touch any software.
