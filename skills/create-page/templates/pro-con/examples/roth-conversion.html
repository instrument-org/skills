Pros and cons

A Roth conversion this year, at 52, in the 24 percent bracket

You have $400,000 in a traditional IRA, you file jointly, and after deductions your taxable income lands near $250,000. The question is not whether to convert but how much and when, and both sides below come down to one comparison: the rate you pay now against the rate you would pay later.

Each point below ends with how much it matters, on a three-step scale: decides it, matters, minor. The weights are set for these three facts; change the facts and they move.

The case for converting

What you gain by paying now

  1. 1

    Two decades of growth that is never taxed again.

    $145,000 converted this year and growing at 6 percent is about $550,000 at 75. Left in the traditional IRA the same money reaches the same figure, but every dollar that comes out is ordinary income.

    How much it matters: decides it. The longer the runway, the more the conversion earns, and 23 years is a long runway.

  2. 2

    Smaller required distributions at 75.

    $400,000 untouched at 6 percent is about $1.5 million at 75, and the first required withdrawal is near $62,000, on top of Social Security and anything else. Every dollar converted now is a dollar that never has to come out on a schedule.

    How much it matters: matters, and more the larger the balance gets.

  3. 3

    A known price for the tax.

    Today's brackets are permanent in law, so no sunset is forcing your hand, but permanent means until Congress changes it. Paying 24 percent now is a certainty; the rate in 2049 is a guess.

    How much it matters: matters, as a hedge. Do not let it choose the amount.

  4. 4

    Access before 59 and a half.

    Converted principal can be withdrawn without penalty five years after each conversion. This year's conversion is reachable at 57, which is useful if you retire early and irrelevant otherwise.

    How much it matters: minor, unless early retirement is the plan.

  5. 5

    Cleaner for whoever inherits it.

    A child inheriting a Roth has ten years to empty it tax-free. Inheriting a traditional IRA means ten years of taxable withdrawals, usually during their own peak earning years.

    How much it matters: minor for you, meaningful for them. Weigh it by whether the money is likely to outlive you.

The case against converting

What paying now costs you

  1. 1

    About $35,000 due in April, from savings.

    Converting to the top of the bracket costs roughly 24 percent of $145,000 in federal tax, plus state. Pay it out of the IRA and you convert less and owe a 10 percent penalty on the withheld part, because you are under 59 and a half.

    How much it matters: decides it. No cash outside the IRA, no conversion this year.

  2. 2

    You will probably be cheaper later.

    Between retiring and 75 there can be a decade of low-income years. A couple living on $110,000 of taxable income sits in the 12 and 22 percent brackets, and a conversion in those years costs less than one now. At 52 those years are most likely ahead of you.

    How much it matters: decides it, in the other direction. This is the whole argument for converting some now and most later.

  3. 3

    It raises this year's income for everything that looks at income.

    Federal student aid looks back two tax years, Medicare premiums look back two years once you reach 63, and marketplace health subsidies use the current year. At 52 the Medicare one is not live yet; the college one may be.

    How much it matters: matters if a child files for aid in the next two years; minor otherwise.

  4. 4

    State tax now rather than later.

    Most states tax a conversion as ordinary income in the year it happens. If you might retire to a state with no income tax, or to one that exempts retirement income, the conversion pays a state tax that waiting would avoid.

    How much it matters: matters in a high-tax state; nothing in Texas or Florida.

  5. 5

    It cannot be undone.

    Undoing a conversion ended in 2018. Convert in January, watch the market fall 20 percent by June, and you have paid tax on value that is gone.

    How much it matters: minor. Converting during a dip is the fix, and the risk shrinks with the amount.

The three numbers that decide it

Good for

A couple with the tax money already in a brokerage account, no student aid forms due in the next two years, and a retirement that looks like the 24 percent bracket or higher because a pension, a rental, or a large balance keeps the income coming.

Bad for

Anyone who would pay the tax out of the IRA, anyone with a child filing for aid, and anyone planning to retire at 58 into a decade of near-zero income, where the same conversions cost 12 to 22 percent instead of 24.

Bottom line

Convert about $145,000 this year, to the top of the 24 percent bracket, and only if the $35,000 comes from a taxable account.

Do not convert the whole $400,000: the top $255,000 would be taxed at 32 and 35 percent, more than you are likely to pay later at any point. Revisit every December, and convert in a down month if one comes. The years between retiring and 75 are where the rest gets converted cheaply.
